# Biobased materials ≠ drop-in replacements

*Vancouver edition (2026-06-08)*

## Provocation

> Biobased materials are often asked to replace fossil-based materials on the same terms: same performance, same price, same infrastructure, same supply chains, same behaviours. That is an extremely difficult game. Incumbent materials are cheap, optimised, standardised, and deeply embedded. The bigger opportunity may be elsewhere.
> 
> Biobased materials may win not by being cheaper plastics, greener cement, or alternative composites, but by offering functions conventional materials cannot easily provide: responsiveness, repair, biodegradation, biological sensing, local production, circularity, adaptation, or integration with living systems.
> 
> Biomaterials should not only ask, “What can we replace?” They should ask, “What can we make possible that was previously impossible?”

## Discussion points

- Where are drop-in replacement strategies useful, and what are they limited by?
- What new functions should define the value of biomaterials?
- How do we build markets, standards and infrastructures for materials that behave differently?
- What would a biobased material look like if it were designed around repair, sensing, locality or circularity rather than replacement? Can we design urban infrastructure for this future?

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**Where are drop-in strategies useful, and what limits them?**
- Fitting into previous moulds limits us; sustainability funding may reinforce this by setting goals for specific applications.
- Biobased materials often need entirely different processing, so they are hard to drop into a complex existing industry.

**What new functions should define the value of biomaterials?**
- Accessibility and ease of fabrication are key to wide integration; composites are often the material of choice, so functionalised biomaterials must compete. What looks good in a paper often differs from what works in industry.
- Reproducibility is a major challenge for advanced functionalised biomaterials: the field must standardise which parameters are shared, and ask how much performance is set by the starting material or supplier, a problem conventional polymers such as polyethylene also have (batch mixing).
- Do not force a material out of its nature: harness natural properties and look at what already exists. Processes and chemistry can themselves act as functions. Purity is a big problem. High-value biomaterials already exist (wood, for example); using waste is attractive environmentally, with function secondary.

**Sustainability claims**
- Claims of sustainability or nature-friendliness are weakened when the functionalisation relies on harsh chemical processes. Sustainability is relative to a reference point, and life-cycle assessment rests on databases whose system boundaries and comparisons keep shifting.
- Fractionation: after diving deep into microstructure, how do we zoom back out and use that knowledge to enable macroscale applications?
