# “Bio” doesn’t make something “good”

*RM003 (2026-04-11)* · notes RM003.1

## Provocation

> "Bio" doesn't make something "good," but biomaterials should be "good". By this, I mean shouldn't something that requires new infrastructures, processes, caring practices, and so on incorporate more ethical considerations than existing materials. If we're making something new, we should make something better - and biology alone is not 'better'.

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**Definitions diverge by field**
- The medical and the fashion/design uses of “biomaterial” may not refer to the same thing: one is defined by manufacture and origin (biologically sourced or derived), the other by use case (biocompatibility). Being explicit about context and use case was recommended.
- In design contexts “biomaterial” may sit closer to *bioreceptive* than to *biocompatible*.

**Different justifications in medicine and design**
- In medical settings sustainability is a minor consideration: little material is used, human health is the “higher cause”, and end of life is already determined (medical waste is incinerated).
- Ethical oversight is bounded more straightforwardly in medicine (clear health-and-safety guidance, COSHH-style forms); ethics is especially pronounced for genetically modified materials and organisms, particularly with human cells.
- From the design side, a new material needs a justification for replacing an existing one: biomaterials invite the reworking of systems and supply chains.

**Care ethics, rules and misuse**
- “Care” can be romanticised in biodesign; in laboratory science it is dictated by protocols (e.g. humane end-points for animals).
- Misuse risk cannot be fully engineered away; it falls to administrative controls and screening (DNA-synthesis providers can flag suspicious orders, but work-arounds exist). Curiosity alone is not a justification for research.
- Rules (safety protocols) are not the same as ethics (personal judgement). Designers working in scientific labs may be assumed to have safety training they lack; “just because you can dye a textile with it” does not make a toxic dye acceptable.

**Concluding thoughts**
- “Bio”, “materials” and “good” are all contested terms; the prompt exposes differences between disciplines.
- For science, “good” was framed as (money >) human health > environment.
- A sense that “good” is unattainable at industrial scale: any process, however much better than the alternative, creates knock-on effects and new patterns of resource use (for example, effluent treatment around a large fermentation-based food plant).
