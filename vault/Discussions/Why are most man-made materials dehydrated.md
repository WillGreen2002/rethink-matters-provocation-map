# Why are most man-made materials “dehydrated”?

*RM003 (2026-04-11)* · notes RM003.2

## Provocation

> Biology exists in the aqueous phase.Why are most man-made materials “dehydrated”? Are biological interfaces (therapies) the only interface suitable for aqueous biomaterials?

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**Main takeaway**
- Humidity in a material is conducive to life, which leads to degradation after fabrication. Above roughly 65% relative humidity, chemical or biological deterioration (hydrolysis, mould) sets in: “biology always wins”. Wetting-and-drying cycles are the particular problem.
- Agency over biodegradation is low: the group felt we have very little control over the moment a material becomes functional and the moment it stops being so.
- Man-made materials are mostly dehydrated (“only as strong as the day they are made”) and tolerate little change in humidity (e.g. swelling and shrinking in wood). There is no built-in cycle of life and death that could keep a living material going without changing its constitution.

**Where aqueous materials might make sense**
- Places that are already wet, or cycle between wet and dry and would do better staying wet: waterlogged timber piles under Venetian buildings; water-filtration membranes; hydrogels; contact lenses and other medical devices (everything biomedical already sits in water, through us).
- Architecture looks more realistic than textiles: wet fabric on skin is unpopular and unhygienic, and garments need washing.

**Wider question**
- Do we want materials to last? “We want total control over end of life”, but that is not currently possible. To what extent can we make things degrade in specific environments?
