# Can a biomaterial be a convivial technology?

*RM004 (2026-04-25)* · notes RM004.3

## Provocation

> Can biomaterial be a Convivial technology?

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**What “convivial” means here**, after Ivan Illich (*Tools for Conviviality*, 1973): tools that let people exercise autonomy and creativity, in contrast to industrial tools that become destructive to autonomy and ingenuity. Examples given: bicycles, kombucha, open-source software (convivial) versus smartphones, cars and closed software (opacity, dependency, isolation, environmental damage). The *Matrix of Convivial Technology* scores a technology on five dimensions (relatedness, access, adaptability, biointeraction, appropriateness) across four life-cycle levels: materials, production, use, infrastructure.

**Takeaways**
- Convivial biomaterials and engineering biology have clear upsides, but access to instruments has to be safely regulated (dangerous chemicals; risks of self-medication).
- Convivial technology can also enable misuse (dual use, bioterrorism, self-accreditation; poor cosmetic-surgery practice was cited). **Safety should be added as an extra row in the matrix.**
- Conviviality might also reduce misuse by making power around technology less asymmetrical: harmful uses can be a product of non-convivial political structures.
- Accessibility versus complexity: simple technologies are easier to make convivial than complex ones (DNA synthesis, for example). That can change through technology choices, or through shared community instrument facilities.
- Safer access needs better regulation and the facilities to comply with it, and building such commons is also a political choice. Some controls already exist: screening of DNA orders, FDA-style approval for foods and drugs, university rules for GMO and pathogens.
- Example: nanoparticle synthesis needs a designated laboratory and proper training; instruments and chemicals can injure the user if mishandled.
