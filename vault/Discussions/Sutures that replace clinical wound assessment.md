# Sutures that replace clinical wound assessment?

*RM005 (2026-05-18)* · notes RM005.7

## Provocation

> Can we make smart enough sutures that will replace clinical wound assessment by clinicians?
> 
> Colour-changing biomaterials will indicate the state of infection, so there will be no need for specialised personnel to monitor the wound. The wound dressing is becoming a diagnostic device (that can also deliver therapeutic agents). Collagen-based fibres can sense changes in pH by changing the colour from healthy acidic wound conditions to an alkaline infected state. This could have a tremendous impact on patients with chronic wounds or severe burns, for example. Can this really happen in the future? Can all wounds be accessed in this way? Can all patients benefit from this?

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**Application issues**
- The chemistry behind the mode of action matters most: a colour-changing dressing is only clinically useful if the signal is specific, reliable and interpretable. pH colour change is easy to engineer.
- Specificity versus regulation: the more generic the signal, the easier to manufacture but the harder to regulate, with a risk of false positives and false negatives. Condition-dependent monitoring is a problem for generic solutions.

**At home versus in the clinic**
- Strong interest in non-invasive diagnostics that limit dressing removal and reapplication. At home: self-detection prompts people to seek care, but misinterpretation, or delayed care if the device fails. In the clinic: bedside diagnostic devices.
- Key question — clinician redundancy: can the device replace clinical judgement? Self-diagnosis is already common (lateral-flow tests, glucose monitors). The idea of “normal” should be objective and measurable but is often subjective.

**Is it feasible on a 10–20–30-year horizon?**
- Animal-derived collagen materials were said to be approved already (citation needed); other candidates include chitosan and cellulose.
- On-demand synthesis at the point of care; multi- or N-dimensional detection (for example click-chemistry-grafted azido-collagen tuned to several conditions, limited by toxicity and compatibility); hydrogels, where release kinetics matter most; nanozymes; nanodiamonds (not yet demonstrated robustly in vivo).
