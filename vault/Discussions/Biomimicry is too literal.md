# Biomimicry is too literal

*Vancouver edition (2026-06-08), RM009 (2026-08-20)* · notes RM009.3

## Provocation

> We often treat biological materials as templates to copy: nacre, bone, wood, spider silk, leaves, skin. But the more radical claim is that biology has already discovered substrate-independent design principles: hierarchy, adaptation, modularity, redundancy, self-assembly, multifunctionality, and damage tolerance achieved through structure rather than composition.
> 
> If those principles are truly portable, they should transfer beyond biological matter into the functional and structural materials we build from scratch, including infrastructure.
> 
> The bottleneck may not be synthesis, characterisation, or fabrication. It may be translation. Materials science, biology, design, and engineering often describe similar phenomena in different languages, at different scales, and with different measures of success.
> 
> Biomimicry is too focused on copying nature’s parts. We should be reverse-engineering nature’s rules.
> 
> Can we extract biological principles in a form that is abstract enough to transfer, but precise enough to design with.

## Discussion points

- What would a shared design language between biology, materials science and infrastructure look like?
- What gets lost when biomimicry becomes visual or metaphorical?

## Post-meeting synthesis (anonymised digest)

**Vancouver session**
- No consensus on the definition of biomimicry. One view: it requires understanding of *mechanism* rather than structure (a materials-science perspective). Another: not necessarily mechanistic understanding, but taking part in wider ecosystem life cycles over time.
- Where is the line between mimicry and inspiration? Mimicry is more precise; “bioinspired” is looser, often a buzzword, and the border is fuzzy. Scale is not the issue; definitions vary by field.
- Are we replicating something, or making something and then finding a similar thing in nature? (Replication example: layer-by-layer, nacre-like fabrication of an organic polymer plus an inorganic mineral. Retrospective-tagging example: a material’s thermal insulation resembling the hollow structure of polar-bear fur.) What matters is the mechanism.
- What gets lost when biomimicry becomes visual or metaphorical? Bioinspiration may fall short of true biomimicry yet still be positive — and still serve as a marketing tool.
- One group argued biomimicry is *not literal enough*: true biomimicry has not been achieved, and symbiosis — water, cell and gas cycles — is the more important concept.

**RM009 follow-up** (brief handwritten notes)
- Better collaboration between the sciences; scale matters, and changes perspective.
- More blue-sky research → support for failure → a more directed agenda.
- Systems biology missed its promise (more sequencing and measurement at different scales); “grey-box” approaches accept that unexpected things happen while still making the unexplained understandable.
